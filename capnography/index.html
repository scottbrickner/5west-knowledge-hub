The physiology
01

Three levers move the number

CORE

EtCO₂ tracks ventilation, but it isn't only a lung number. It's what's left after the body makes CO₂, the blood carries it, and the lungs dump it.

M

Metabolism

Makes the CO₂
↓ EtCO₂Hypothermia, sedation, analgesia, paralysis, NPO
↑ EtCO₂Fever, shivering, pain, seizures, sepsis, malignant hyperthermia
P

Perfusion

Delivers the CO₂ to the lungs
↓ EtCO₂Low cardiac output, hypovolemia, hemorrhage, massive PE, cardiac arrest
↑ EtCO₂Cardiac output restored with ventilation unchanged (ROSC, after a fluid bolus)
V

Ventilation

Removes the CO₂
↓ EtCO₂Hyperventilation (rate or tidal volume up), anxiety, pain
↑ EtCO₂Hypoventilation, lower vent rate, oversedation, COPD

The shape
02

Waveform anatomy

CORE

Every abnormal pattern is a normal breath with one piece broken. Learn the four pieces.

The gap

EtCO₂ estimates PaCO₂. It doesn't replace it.

Exhaled gas from working alveoli mixes with gas from dead space (airways and alveoli that get air but no blood). That dilutes the CO₂, so PaCO₂ is normally a few mmHg higher than EtCO₂. Get an ABG to see how they line up for this patient, then trend the EtCO₂.

Normal gap

2–5 mmHg

PaCO₂ slightly above EtCO₂.

Gap widens when

Dead space goes up: low cardiac output, PE, hypovolemia, high PEEP, COPD, ARDS, or a leak in the sampling system or around the tube.

So what

A "normal" EtCO₂ can hide a high PaCO₂. If the patient doesn't match the number, trust the patient and check an ABG.

How it measures

What the sensor sees, and when to trust it

The whole breath

Capnography measures CO₂ through every part of the breath, inspiration included. The EtCO₂ number is the peak at the end of exhalation, and the monitor counts each waveform to give the airway respiratory rate (awRR). It updates breath by breath, in real time.

Infrared absorption

CO₂ absorbs infrared light. The sensor shines infrared through the gas and measures how much light gets through: the less that passes, the more CO₂ in that breath.

Mainstream

The sensor sits right on the airway adapter. Fastest reading, no sample line to clog. The tradeoff: it's built for intubated or bagged airways, and its weight at the tube can tug on the ETT. RT places it on anyone being bagged.

Sidestream / Microstream

A thin line pulls a gas sample back to the monitor. Light at the airway and works on non-intubated patients through a sampling nasal cannula. The tradeoffs: a short delay, and water or secretions can block the line.

Trust the wave before the number

An EtCO₂ value is only valid when the waveform behind it looks right: a clean upstroke and a real plateau. No good wave, no good number; fix the cause before you chart it. Keep the adapter's sampling port and sensor pointing up so condensation and secretions drain away from it, and never flush the sampling line.

At the bedside
04

See · Think · Do

CORE

Look at the patient before the monitor. SpO₂ can stay normal for minutes after a tube comes out; EtCO₂ drops in one breath.

Every intubation, every tube move

Continuous waveform capnography is how tube placement is confirmed and watched. After intubation, a reintubation, or a tube move, look for a sustained waveform breath after breath. A wave that fades away over a few breaths, or never appears, means the tube is not in the trachea until proven otherwise. After every turn or transport, check that the wave is still there.

Waveform suddenly gone

Act now

See: flat line at zero, EtCO₂ reads 0 or blank, apnea alarm. SpO₂ may still look fine.

Think: tube dislodged or in the esophagus, circuit disconnected, tube completely blocked, apnea, cardiac arrest. Sampling failure comes last.

  1. Look at the patient: chest rise, breath sounds, color, pulse.
  2. Check the circuit from the patient back: connections, then the tube depth (cm at the teeth or lip) against the documented depth.
  3. Can't confirm the tube? Disconnect from the vent, bag with 100% O₂, watch for a waveform on the bag.
  4. Still no waveform: call RT and the provider STAT; call a code if pulseless.
Think DOPEDisplacement · Obstruction · Pneumothorax · Equipment

EtCO₂ falling fast, waveform still there

Act now

See: the shape is normal, but each breath is shorter than the one before, over a few minutes.

Think: the perfusion lever. Cardiac arrest, massive PE, sudden hemorrhage, profound hypotension.

  1. Check pulse, BP, and rhythm now.
  2. Pulseless: start CPR and call a code.
  3. Pulse present: assess for bleeding (chest tube output, drains, sites) and notify the provider.
Why it worksCO₂ is still being made, but the blood isn't bringing it to the lungs.

Shark fin

Act now

See: slow, sloped upstroke that never flattens into a plateau. Often comes with high peak pressures.

Think: obstruction to exhalation. Bronchospasm, secretions, kinked or bitten tube, auto-PEEP, COPD.

  1. Check the tube for kinks or biting; place a bite block if needed.
  2. Listen: wheezes or coarse sounds? Suction if indicated.
  3. Check peak pressures and alarms on the vent.
  4. Call RT for bronchodilator / vent assessment; notify the provider if it's not resolving.
Shape first, number secondPartial obstruction changes the shape before it changes the number. EtCO₂ may read normal or even rise, so don't let a normal number talk you out of a fin.

Gradual rise

Fix & watch

See: normal shape, plateau creeping up over minutes to hours.

Think: ventilation down (vent rate lowered, oversedation on a spontaneous mode) or metabolism up (fever, shivering, sepsis, bicarb given). Rapid, unexplained rise with rigidity and fever: malignant hyperthermia.

  1. Check vent mode, set vs. actual rate, tidal volume.
  2. Check temperature; treat shivering and fever per orders.
  3. Get an ABG to compare against PaCO₂.
  4. Notify the provider per the order's parameters. CP 3-170
AskWhich lever moved: metabolism or ventilation?

Gradual fall

Fix & watch

See: normal shape, plateau drifting down.

Think: hyperventilation (pain, anxiety, vent rate up), falling cardiac output, hypothermia, or a leak.

  1. Check perfusion first: BP, HR, cap refill, urine output. Low output is the dangerous cause.
  2. Then pain, anxiety, sedation, vent settings.
  3. Check the cuff and circuit for a leak.
TrapA low EtCO₂ is not proof of a low PaCO₂. If perfusion dropped, the gap widened.

Baseline doesn't return to zero

Fix & watch

See: the inspiratory part of the wave sits above zero and may keep climbing.

Think: rebreathing CO₂. Faulty exhalation valve, too little expiratory time, water or contamination in the sampling line or sensor.

  1. Call RT to check the circuit and exhalation valve.
  2. Check the sampling line and filter for water. Never flush the sampling line.
  3. Rezero or replace the sensor per the manufacturer.
Sidestream tipWater traps and filters exist for a reason; change them, don't flush them.

Curare cleft

Fix & watch

See: a notch dipping into the plateau.

Think: the patient is breathing on their own against the vent. Paralytic or sedation wearing off, dyssynchrony.

  1. Assess comfort, sedation level, and synchrony with the vent.
  2. On a neuromuscular blocker? Check train-of-four per order and notify the provider.
  3. Call RT for vent synchrony.
Good news sometimesDuring a sedation wean, a cleft can just mean the patient is waking up.

Low, ragged plateau

Fix & watch

See: the plateau sags or drops off early, values bounce breath to breath.

Think: a leak. Cuff leak (often audible at the mouth), loose or cracked sampling line, water in the line.

  1. Listen at the mouth for a cuff leak; call RT to check cuff pressure.
  2. Check every connection from the adapter to the monitor.
Don'tchart a leak value as the patient's EtCO₂.
Beyond the vent

Capnography isn't just for intubated patients

CP 3-170 covers intubated patients. You'll still meet capnography outside the vent, so know where else it earns its place.

Non-intubated patients

Sampling nasal cannulas give valid readings and waveforms in spontaneously breathing patients, in the ICU, the procedural areas and on the floors.

Opioids & sedation

Recommended for patients on high-dose opioids, PCA, or procedural sedation. It catches respiratory depression well before SpO₂ falls, especially when the patient is on supplemental oxygen.

Feeding & NG tubes

A CO₂ waveform through a newly placed gastric tube means it's in the airway. Capnography can help guide placement; follow the placement policy for final confirmation.

Resuscitation
05

CPR & ROSC

DEEPER

In a code, EtCO₂ is a cardiac output monitor. Better compressions push more CO₂-rich blood through the lungs.

Coach compressions

≥ 10

mmHg at minimum, ideally ≥ 20. Below 10: push harder, faster, fully recoil, or swap compressors.

Catch ROSC

Abrupt ↑

A sudden, sustained jump (often 10+ mmHg) can mean ROSC. Confirm at the next rhythm/pulse check; don't stop CPR early to look.

Never alone

1 of many

Per AHA 2025, EtCO₂ should not be used by itself to decide to stop resuscitation.

Read the bumps carefully

Sodium bicarbonate releases CO₂ and causes a short spike that isn't ROSC. Epinephrine can dip EtCO₂ briefly. Hyperventilating with the bag also lowers it. Read the number together with what was just done.

Try it

Run the code

Time is compressed: 10 seconds = 1 minute. Compressors fatigue after about 2 minutes. Keep EtCO₂ above the lines.

Keck policy

Keck policy crosswalk

CORE

This page teaches the concepts. Keck's numbers live in the policy: open it (Keck sign-in) before you set alarms or chart.

CP 3-170 · End-Tidal CO₂ (Capnography) Monitoring of Intubated Patients ↗

Ordering

Capnography starts with a provider order, and the order must say when to notify the provider.

Normal range & alarm limits

Keck's normal EtCO₂ range and baseline alarm limits are set in the policy. Use those, not the textbook 35–45.

Documentation

The policy sets how often EtCO₂ is charted, plus PRN with significant changes or per order.

ABG correlation

Get an ABG after capnography is placed to document how EtCO₂ and PaCO₂ line up.

Sampling line care

Never flush the sampling line; it damages the filter and lets moisture into the monitor.

Manual ventilation & codes

RT places mainstream EtCO₂ on patients being bagged, including before intubation. See also CP 3-123 Code Blue Response ↗
